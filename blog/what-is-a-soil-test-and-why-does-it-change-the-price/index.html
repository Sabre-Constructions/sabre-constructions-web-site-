What is a soil test, and why does
it change the price of a new house?

It's a few holes drilled in the yard and a report with a letter on it, and it moves the price of everything below the floor. Here's what a soil test is, what the classes mean, why Bayside ground is what it is, and why a quote written before the test is a guess.

Nathan Brain, carpenter and builder at Sabre 17 September 2026 5 min read

The short answer

A soil test is a geotechnical investigation of your block: a few boreholes, samples taken and tested, and a report that classifies the site under AS 2870, the Australian Standard for residential slabs and footings. The classes run from A (stable sand or rock) through S, M, H1 and H2 (slightly to highly reactive clay) to E (extremely reactive) and P (problem sites, including filled ground).

Reactive clay swells when wet and shrinks when dry, and the house has to ride that without cracking. The higher the class, the stiffer and deeper the footings and slab have to be, and the more they cost. That's why the letter on the report changes the price.

The test is done before the engineer designs the footings and before a builder can fix the price of them. A quote written without it has an allowance where the footings should be.

What actually happens in a soil test?

A geotechnical technician drills boreholes at points across the block, usually where the house will sit, and logs what comes up: topsoil, fill, clay, sand, rock, and the depth of each. Samples go to a laboratory to measure how much the clay moves with moisture.

The report classifies the site, notes the depth to firm ground and the water table, flags fill or old footings, and gives the engineer what they need to design the slab and footings. It's a short document with a long effect.

What do the classes mean?

AS 2870 sorts sites by expected ground movement:

  • A — stable sand or rock. Little movement. The simplest footings.
  • S — slightly reactive clay. Slight movement.
  • M — moderately reactive. Moderate movement; a common class in Brisbane.
  • H1 and H2 — highly reactive clay. High to very high movement. Stiffened or waffle raft slabs and deeper footings.
  • E — extremely reactive. Extreme movement; engineered solutions throughout.
  • P — problem site. Fill, soft ground, unusual water conditions, or ground that varies across the block. Each one needs its own engineering.

AS 2870 classifies residential sites by their predicted characteristic surface movement — the expected vertical ground movement between fully dry and fully wet — and prescribes footing and slab systems for each class.

Standards Australia, AS 2870 Residential slabs and footings. The class is an engineering input, not an opinion, and every certifier and engineer in Queensland works to it.

What is Bayside ground usually like?

Varied, which is the honest answer, and the reason the test matters here.

Close to the bay there's sand, and old filled ground where the foreshore and the creek flats were built up. Further back the clays begin, and on the acreage edge toward Ransome and Gumdale there are both. A block can change class from the front fence to the back. Old houses on the Bayside show it: the ones with a cracked slab or a door that never shut properly are often on clay that was never accounted for.

We won't tell you your class from the street. Nobody can. That's what the drill is for.

Why does it change the price?

Because the footings are designed to the class, and the footings are concrete, steel, excavation and time.

A class A or S site can take a standard slab. An H or E site needs a stiffer slab with deeper edge beams, more reinforcement, sometimes piers to firm ground, and more careful drainage around the house so the moisture in the clay stays even. A P site with old fill might need the fill removed and replaced, or piers through it.

None of that is optional, and none of it is visible when the house is finished. It's also the part of the price that a “from” figure for a house doesn't include, because the house doesn't know your ground. The other things that move the price are here.

When is it done, and who orders it?

Early. On a knockdown rebuild it can be done with the old house still standing, drilled in the yard around it, and the results feed the design before the slab is drawn. The builder or the designer usually orders it, and a geotechnical firm does the work.

If a builder hands you a fixed price for footings before the test is in, ask what class they've assumed. If they've assumed the best case, the price will move.

Key takeaways

  • A soil test classifies your ground under AS 2870, from A to P.
  • Reactive clay moves with moisture, and the footings are designed to ride it.
  • Higher class, stiffer and deeper footings, higher cost. That's the link to the price.
  • Bayside ground varies: sand and fill near the water, clay behind it, both on the acreage edge.
  • Get it done early, before the design is fixed. A footing price without it is an assumption.

Common questions

Can we use the neighbour's soil test?

As a hint, not a design. Ground changes across a fence line, and the engineer needs bores on your lot where your house will sit. The certifier will expect a report for your address.

Does a bad class mean we shouldn't build?

No. Houses are built on H, E and P sites every week in Brisbane. It means the engineering is heavier and the price reflects it. What you don't want is to find out after the slab is priced.

Does the old house's cracking tell us anything?

It's a clue, and it's why a builder looks under the old house at the walk-through. Cracked slabs, stumps that have moved and doors that bind all point to reactive ground. The test confirms it.

Who pays for the soil test?

It's part of the job, and how it's billed depends on the builder and the stage. Ask. What matters is that it's done before the footing price is fixed.
