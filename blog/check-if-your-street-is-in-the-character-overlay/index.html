How to check if your house
is in the character overlay.

Two free checks and about fifteen minutes. One tells you whether your street is protected. The other tells you the year your house went up. You need both answers, and you can get them yourself this weekend.

Nathan Brain, carpenter and builder at Sabre 10 September 2026 6 min read

The short answer

Two free checks and about fifteen minutes will tell you where you stand.

The first is Brisbane City Council's City Plan online mapping. Type in your address, turn on the overlays, and it tells you whether your property sits in the Traditional building character overlay.

The second is the year your house was built, which the mapping won't always tell you. For that you want Brisbane City Archives, who hold building records going back to 1904.

You need both answers, because the rules only bite where a pre-1947 house and the overlay meet. A 1960s house inside the overlay is a different situation from a 1930s house inside it, and a 1930s house outside the overlay is different again. Neither check costs anything, and you can do both before you talk to anybody.

Check one: is your property in the overlay?

Council publishes the whole planning scheme, maps included, in a tool called City Plan online.

  1. Go to cityplan.brisbane.qld.gov.au/eplan and type your address into the search.
  2. Open the map for your property.
  3. In the Maps Tools panel, turn on the Overlays layers. The one you're looking for is Traditional building character.
  4. Generate the property report. It's a short summary of the City Plan requirements that apply to your site, and it lists the overlays affecting your lot in one place.

Two things people get wrong here.

  • It's mapped street by street, not suburb by suburb. One side of a road can be in the overlay and the other side not. What the neighbours did tells you nothing reliable about your block.
  • “In the overlay” is not the same as “can't be touched.” The overlay is about the look of the street. It's what makes demolition of a pre-1947 house something council decides on, rather than something you simply do. The rules, and the limited situations where a pre-1947 house can still come down, are here: Can we knock down our house?

If you'd rather ask a person, council's general number is 07 3403 8888.

The Traditional building character overlay exists “to maintain the traditional, pre-1947 streetscape.”

Brisbane City Council, Brisbane City Plan 2014. That one line is why the year your house was built matters as much as the map does — the overlay protects a streetscape, and the line it draws is 1946.

Check two: what year did your house go up?

This is the question most families can't answer about their own home, and it matters more than the overlay does.

The line is 1946. Built in 1946 or earlier, and the character rules are in play if your street is in the overlay. Built after, and they almost certainly aren't.

Council has a practice note on exactly this — determining the construction date of a building — because it comes up constantly. The records themselves sit with Brisbane City Archives:

  • Building Application Registers run from March 1904 to October 1945, arranged by year and indexed by street and by owner's name.
  • From November 1945, every building application went onto an individual property card. Those cards record new houses as well as extensions and alterations, and they're searchable.

There are no surviving registers for September 1934 to January 1936 or April to December 1937, and for 1939 and 1941 only the index survives.

Brisbane City Archives, building application registers. If your house went up in one of those windows, a missing record isn't evidence the house isn't pre-1947 — it's evidence the paperwork didn't survive. Worth knowing before you conclude anything.

The Archives are at Moorooka and you can ring them on 07 3403 1711.

Other things that help date a house:

  • The title.
  • Historical aerial photography.
  • The house itself. Stumps, VJ walls, casement windows and a particular kind of roof line tell a builder a lot in about thirty seconds — one reason a walk-through is worth more than a phone call.

What do you do with the two answers?

Four combinations, and they lead different places.

Your houseYour streetWhere that leaves you
Built after 1946Not in the overlayThe character rules aren't your issue. Normal approvals still apply
Built after 1946In the overlayUsually fine for the house itself, but the overlay still shapes what a new house looks like from the street
Built 1946 or earlierNot in the overlayThe pre-1947 protections are tied to the overlay, so this is a much simpler conversation than people expect
Built 1946 or earlierIn the overlayDemolition needs a development application and council decides. Not automatically no. Not automatically yes

If you land in the bottom row, don't assume the answer is no and don't assume it's yes. It's a case to be made, with evidence. Get a builder and a town planner to look at it before you either give up on the block or spend money as though approval is a formality.

And if the house was built before 1911, council's position is that it's kept. That's the one row where the answer is usually the end of the conversation for a full knockdown.

Why do we check this first, every time?

Because the alternative is a family spending months and money on a design for a house that can't be built — or walking away from a block that would have been fine.

It's the first thing we look at, it costs nothing, and you can do it yourself this weekend.

Key takeaways

  • Two free checks, fifteen minutes. Council's City Plan mapping for the overlay; Brisbane City Archives for the year.
  • The overlay is mapped street by street. Your neighbour's block tells you nothing reliable about yours.
  • The year matters as much as the map. The line is 1946. After it, the character rules almost certainly don't apply.
  • A missing archive record is not an answer. Registers are lost for parts of 1934–37, 1939 and 1941.
  • Pre-1947 and in the overlay means council decides. Not automatically no, not automatically yes — a case to be made with evidence.

Common questions

Does the overlay stop me renovating?

No. It shapes what work on a pre-1947 house looks like from the street, and it's why some changes need approval that you'd assume wouldn't. Renovating inside the overlay happens all the time.

Is the map ever wrong?

The map is council's, and it changes from time to time. It's the source of truth on the day you read it, which is exactly why you check it yourself rather than relying on what someone told you about the street three years ago.

My neighbour knocked theirs down. Doesn't that settle it?

No. The overlay is mapped street by street, their house may have been a different year, and their application may have been decided on things specific to their house. It's a good sign, not an answer.
