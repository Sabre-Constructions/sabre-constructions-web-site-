What happens to the trees on the
block in a knockdown rebuild?

The big gum in the backyard is either the best thing about your block or the reason the design has to bend. In Brisbane, that isn't always your call. Here's how council protects trees on private land, how to check yours in a few minutes, and what it does to a rebuild.

Nathan Brain, carpenter and builder at Sabre 17 September 2026 5 min read

The short answer

Some of the trees on your block may be protected, and if they are, they stay unless council issues a permit. Brisbane City Council's Natural Assets Local Law 2003 protects four kinds of vegetation: council's own (street trees, parks), vegetation along waterways and wetlands, significant urban vegetation (mature or prominent trees on private land, native or not), and significant native vegetation on mapped properties.

Council's words: penalties apply if you interfere with protected vegetation without a permit. That includes lopping, not just felling, and it applies on your own land.

You can ask council for a protected vegetation report on your property and get a map back. A builder designs around what it shows. On the Bayside, with its creeks, mangroves and old street trees, that report is worth reading before a plan is drawn.

Which trees are protected?

Council's local law names four categories, and two of them sit on private land.

  • Council vegetation. Street trees, park trees, vegetation in natural areas. The tree on the verge in front of your house is council's, not yours.
  • Waterway and wetland vegetation. Along the creeks and the foreshore, fresh or salt, natural or man-made. Lota Creek, the Wynnum creeks and the mangrove edges all count.
  • Significant urban vegetation. Council's words: native and exotic vegetation on private property that is mature and/or prominent in the landscape. The big fig, the old camphor laurel, the gum everyone can see from the street.
  • Significant native vegetation. All native vegetation on properties in council's mapping, from ground covers to large trees.

Council also lets residents nominate a tree or a group of trees for a vegetation protection order. Old Bayside streets have a few.

“Penalties apply if you interfere with protected vegetation without a permit.” A property may have protections if it is near or in a waterway corridor, in a bushland area, contains large significant trees even in an urban area, or has heritage values.

Brisbane City Council, Protected vegetation (Natural Assets Local Law 2003). It's the owner's job to know, and the map is free.

How do you check your block?

Two ways, both from council.

  1. Open council's community maps (the land and environmental map) and look up your address. The protected vegetation layers show on the lot.
  2. Make a protected vegetation enquiry about your property. Council sends back a map of protected vegetation on the lot and a report as a PDF.

Do the second one. The report is what a designer and a certifier want to see, and it's what you'd need to apply for a permit anyway.

What if a protected tree is where the house goes?

Then you apply to council for a permit, and the design might change first.

Council's guidance is to talk to them about your plans before lodging a development application, and to apply for the vegetation permit after the DA is finalised. In practice that means the tree is part of the design conversation from the start, not a surprise at demolition. A permit isn't automatic. Council can refuse, or condition it, or ask for the design to move.

There's also the City Plan side: a lot under the significant landscape tree overlay is code assessable, which means a new house needs a development application. That post explains the two approvals.

What about the trees that aren't protected?

Most trees on most suburban blocks aren't in any of the four categories, and those are yours to keep or remove. Check the report before you assume, and check the verge: the street tree is council's even when it's your problem.

Removal before demolition is a job for an arborist or the demolition contractor, and the stumps and roots come out before anyone digs footings.

What does a builder do with a kept tree?

Designs around it, then protects it.

A big tree decides where the house can sit, where the driveway goes, and what the footings near it have to do. Roots don't stop at the drip line. The arborist's advice about the protection zone becomes part of the site plan, the tree gets fenced before the excavator arrives, and nothing is stored or parked under it for the length of the job.

It's more work. It's also usually why the block was worth staying on.

Key takeaways

  • Brisbane protects trees on private land under the Natural Assets Local Law 2003 — mature or prominent ones, native or not, plus waterway and mapped native vegetation.
  • It's an offence to touch a protected tree without a permit, including lopping.
  • Ask council for a protected vegetation report on your lot. It comes back as a map and a PDF.
  • Talk to council before the DA, apply for the permit after it. Council's own order.
  • A kept tree shapes the design and gets fenced on site. That's the builder's job.

Common questions

Can we remove a tree on the footpath that's in the way of the driveway?

Not without council. Street trees are council vegetation under the local law. Talk to council about the crossover and the tree together; sometimes the driveway moves instead.

The tree is dangerous. Do we still need a permit?

Ask council before you act. There are processes for dangerous or dead trees, and an arborist's report is usually the first step. Cutting first and explaining later is exactly what the penalties are for.

Does a protected tree stop the rebuild?

Rarely. It moves the house, the driveway or the footings. Houses get built around big trees all over the Bayside, and often the tree is the reason the family stayed.

Who fences the tree during the build?

The builder, before demolition starts, to the protection zone an arborist sets. It stays fenced until handover.
