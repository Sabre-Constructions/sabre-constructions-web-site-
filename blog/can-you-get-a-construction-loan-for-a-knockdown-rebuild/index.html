Your questions answered · Dealing with a builder

Can you get a construction loan
for a knockdown rebuild?

We're builders, not lenders, and this post is careful about that. What we can tell you is the shape of the thing: how a construction loan pays for a house differently from a mortgage on one that already exists, what that means for the contract you sign with us, and the three things lenders will ask you for that a builder can help with. The rest is a conversation with a broker or a bank.

Nathan Brain, carpenter and builder at Sabre 17 September 2026 5 min read

The short answer

Generally, yes. Lenders offer construction loans for building a new house on land you own, and a knockdown rebuild is that with a demolition at the front. Whether you can get one, for how much, and on what terms, is a decision for a lender or a mortgage broker, based on your finances. We're builders. We don't know, and we won't guess.

What we can describe is the shape. A construction loan usually isn't paid to you in a lump. The lender pays the builder in stages as the house goes up, after its own valuer confirms the stage is done. The loan is usually sized on the value of the land plus the finished house, and it usually wants a fixed-price contract, plans and specifications, and a stage payment schedule before it's approved.

That shape lines up neatly with Queensland's rules on progress payments, which already require claims to match work done on site. A fixed-price contract with a sensible stage schedule is what both the law and the lender want.

What does a construction loan look like?

In general terms, and every lender is different:

  • Staged drawdowns. The loan is released in parts that match the builder's stage claims — base, frame, lock-up, fixing, completion, or whatever the contract names.
  • The lender's valuer. Before each stage is paid, the lender typically sends a valuer to confirm the work is done. That's a second check, alongside the certifier, that a claim matches the house.
  • Valuation on completion. The lender usually values the project as if complete — land plus finished house — and lends against that.
  • Paperwork up front. A signed fixed-price building contract, council-approved plans and specifications, and the stage schedule are commonly required before approval.

What's different about a knockdown rebuild?

Three things a lender tends to notice.

The house that's there now goes away. If there's a mortgage on the existing house, the security changes when it's demolished. Lenders have processes for this; it's a conversation to have before demolition, not after.

There's a demolition and site stage before the build. Asbestos removal, demolition and disconnections are real costs before the first stage of the new house. Some lenders treat them as a stage; some expect them to be funded another way. Ask.

You're living somewhere else. Rent during the build isn't in the building contract and lenders know it's there. Have your plan for it.

What will a lender ask the builder for?

  • A fixed-price building contract, signed, with the price on the front page as Schedule 1B requires.
  • Plans and specifications, and the building approval once issued.
  • The stage payment schedule from the contract.
  • The builder's QBCC licence details and, often, evidence of the Home Warranty cover.
  • Sometimes a builder's questionnaire or the lender's own form.

Every one of those is a normal thing for a builder to produce, and a builder who can't is telling you something.

Progress claims under a Queensland domestic building contract must be “directly related to work progress on site and proportionate to (or less than) the value of the work that relates to the claim.”

Queensland Building and Construction Commission, Consumer Building Guide, version 3, effective July 2023. The lender's valuer and the QBCC's rule are asking the same question of every claim.

What does it mean for the contract?

That a fixed price with few allowances isn't just the safer contract for you; it's usually the one a lender will fund. Cost-plus contracts, with no fixed total, are harder to lend against as well as riskier to sign. And the stage schedule in the contract should be one a valuer can walk up and verify: a frame stage means a frame you can see.

It also means the timeline has a second audience. Variations that move the price, and extensions that move the date, may need to be communicated to the lender too.

Who decides, and when to talk to them

A mortgage broker or your lender, and early — before the design is finished, ideally before the walk-through. Knowing what you can borrow shapes what gets drawn. Bring them the block, the rough brief and the fact that it's a knockdown; bring us their questions about the contract and the stages. We'll answer the building ones. We won't answer the lending ones, because we'd be guessing.

Key takeaways

  • Construction loans for knockdown rebuilds exist. Whether you get one is a lender's decision, not ours.
  • The shape: staged payments to the builder, the lender's valuer at each stage, sized on land plus finished house.
  • A knockdown adds three wrinkles: the existing mortgage, the demolition stage, and rent during the build.
  • Lenders want the fixed-price contract, plans, stage schedule and licence. All normal for a builder to supply.
  • Talk to a broker or lender early, before the design is fixed.

Common questions

Can Sabre recommend a broker or a bank?

We'd rather not steer you. Ask people you trust, and ask any broker how they're paid. What we'll do is give whoever you choose the building paperwork quickly and completely.

Does the lender pay the deposit?

Ask them. The deposit under the contract is capped by law at 5% for contracts of $20,000 or more, and how it's funded is between you and your lender.

What if the valuer disagrees with a stage claim?

Then the stage isn't paid until it's resolved, which is the system working. The certifier's inspection certificate for the stage usually settles it.

Will you talk to our broker directly?

Yes, with your say-so, about the contract, the stages and the timeline. Not about your finances, which we don't need to know.
