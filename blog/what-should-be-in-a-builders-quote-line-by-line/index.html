What should be in a builder's quote,
line by line?

Two quotes for the same house can be a long way apart, and the difference is usually in what one of them left out. Here's what a complete quote for a knockdown rebuild should have in it, line by line, so you can compare like with like and see where a number is thin before you sign.

Nathan Brain, carpenter and builder at Sabre 17 September 2026 5 min read

The short answer

A complete quote for a knockdown rebuild has five groups of lines: before the house (asbestos removal, demolition, disconnections, the soil test, engineering, design, approvals and the certifier); the site (earthworks, retaining, drainage, the services connections); the structure (footings and slab or subfloor, frame, roof, external walls and windows); the inside (plumbing, electrical, linings, joinery, tiling, painting, floors, fit-off); and the outside and the paperwork (driveway, fencing, landscaping, the Home Warranty premium, insurances, the builder's margin and GST).

Then, separately and clearly, the allowances and provisional sums: the items the price can't fix yet, each with its estimate. The QBCC's guide says these should be kept to a minimum because the final cost often exceeds the estimate.

The three places a quote is usually thin: the site (earthworks and retaining), the allowances (a long list of them), and what's simply not there (the driveway, the landscaping, the certifier). Compare the lines, not the totals.

Before the house

  • Asbestos removal by a licensed contractor, with disposal.
  • Demolition and site clearance, including old slabs, footings, sheds, the driveway.
  • Disconnections: power, sewer capping by a licensed plumber, gas.
  • Soil test and structural engineering for the footings and frame.
  • Design and drawings, and any development application the overlays require.
  • The certifier's fees for the building approval and the mandatory inspections.

Some builders show these as a separate “preliminaries” or “site” section; some leave demolition and asbestos to you. Either is fine as long as it's written down. What isn't fine is a quote that doesn't mention them.

The site

  • Earthworks: cut and fill, and the removal of spoil.
  • Retaining walls, engineered where they hold a slope.
  • Stormwater drainage to the street or the easement, and any on-site detention required.
  • Service connections: sewer, water, power to the new house, and the temporary builder's supply.
  • Temporary fencing, site toilet, skips and the like.

On a sloping Bayside block the site section is where the honest quote and the optimistic quote part company. Why is here.

The structure

  • Footings and slab to the engineer's design for the soil class, with termite management and the membrane; or posts, bearers and floor frame on a highset.
  • Wall and roof frame, timber or steel, with bracing and tie-downs.
  • Roofing, gutters, downpipes, fascia.
  • External walls: cladding, brick, render, as specified.
  • Windows and external doors, with the glazing certificate the certifier wants at the end.
  • Stairs, decks and balustrades.

The inside

  • Plumbing and drainage, and the hot water system.
  • Electrical, switchboard, lighting, data, smoke alarms.
  • Insulation to the energy efficiency requirements.
  • Internal linings, cornices, doors, architraves and skirtings.
  • Waterproofing to the wet areas, with its certificate.
  • Joinery: kitchen, bathrooms, laundry, wardrobes.
  • Tiling, painting, floor coverings, fit-off (tapware, fittings, appliances if included).

The outside and the paperwork

  • Driveway and crossover, paths, clothesline, letterbox.
  • Fencing and basic landscaping, or a clear statement that they're excluded.
  • The Home Warranty premium, which the builder collects and pays to the QBCC.
  • Insurances: contract works, public liability.
  • The builder's margin and GST, and whether the total includes them.

The allowances

Listed separately, each with its estimate: tiles, tapware, appliances, lighting, sometimes the site works if the soil test isn't in yet. This is the part of the quote that behaves like an estimate rather than a price, and the QBCC's advice is direct.

“Allowances (items or services for which the price is not fixed when the contract is signed) should be kept to a minimum as the final cost often exceeds the estimate in the contract.” The QBCC's checklist asks you to check what proportion of the total is allowances.

Queensland Building and Construction Commission, Consumer Building Guide, version 3, effective July 2023. Count them. Ask why each one is there.

Where a quote is usually thin

  • The site. Earthworks and retaining priced as if the block were flat, or as an allowance.
  • The allowances. A cheap quote is often a quote with the real prices moved into a long allowance list.
  • What's missing. No driveway, no fencing, no certifier, no Home Warranty line, no GST line. Every one of those turns up later as a cost.

The check is simple: put two quotes side by side and tick the lines above against each. The cheaper total is usually the shorter list.

Key takeaways

  • Five groups of lines: before the house, the site, the structure, the inside, the outside and the paperwork.
  • Allowances listed separately with their estimates. Keep them few; the QBCC says why.
  • A quote is usually thin in three places: the site, the allowances, and what it doesn't mention.
  • Compare the lines, not the totals. The cheaper total is usually the shorter list.
  • The quote becomes the contract's price and specification. If it isn't in there, it isn't in the price.

Common questions

Should the quote have a total on the front?

The contract must, if the price is fixed: prominently, on the first page of the schedule. The quote is the working document that gets you there, and it should be clear whether its total includes GST and the allowances.

Is a longer quote always better?

A more complete one is. Length for its own sake isn't the point; the point is that every line above has an answer, even if the answer is “excluded, you're arranging it.”

Why do two builders' allowances differ so much?

Because an allowance is a guess about what you'll choose. One builder guesses low to win the job; another guesses at what families actually pick. Ask each one what their allowance would buy, in a shop you can walk into.
