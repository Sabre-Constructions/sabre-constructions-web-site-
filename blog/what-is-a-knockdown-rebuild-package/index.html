What is a knockdown
rebuild package?

A package starts with the house and fits it to your block. A custom build starts with your block and your family, and draws the house to them. Neither is the right answer for everybody — but you should know which one you're being offered.

The short answer

A knockdown rebuild package is a builder's existing house design, sold together with the demolition of your old house, at an advertised price. You choose a plan from a range, they check it fits your block, and you build that plan. It's a genuinely good way to build if one of the plans suits how your family lives and your block is straightforward, because the design work is already done and the price is known earlier.

The trade is direction. A package starts with the house and fits it to the block. A custom build starts with the block and the family and draws the house to them. Neither is the right answer for everybody. What matters is knowing which one you're being offered, and what the advertised number does and doesn't include.

What's usually in a package?

It varies enormously between builders, which is the single most useful thing to know about them. Broadly, a knockdown rebuild package tends to bundle:

  • Demolition of the existing house, sometimes as an allowance rather than a fixed figure
  • A house from the builder's range, at a stated size and configuration
  • A defined level of finish — a standard specification, with upgrades priced separately
  • Some of the approvals and site work, to a stated set of assumptions about the block

The phrase to watch is to a stated set of assumptions. Advertised package pricing generally assumes a straightforward site: manageable slope, ordinary soil, room for a truck, no surprises in the old house. Those are reasonable assumptions on plenty of blocks. They're just assumptions, and your block either matches them or it doesn't.

If you're weighing up a package, the honest version of the conversation is: what happens to this price when the soil test comes back, and when the demolisher opens up the old house? Ask it early, ask it plainly, and get the answer in writing.

Who does a package genuinely suit?

Plenty of families, and it's worth saying so plainly.

A package is a good fit when:

  • One of the plans genuinely works for how your family lives — not nearly, actually.
  • Your block is regular. Flat or close to it, decent access, nothing unusual running through it.
  • You'd rather decide less. A defined range and a standard specification is fewer decisions, and for some families that's the feature, not the compromise.
  • Knowing a number early matters more than getting exactly the house you pictured.

If that's you, a package can be a sensible, efficient way to end up in a good house. Nobody at Sabre thinks otherwise.

Where does it stop suiting people?

Usually in one of three places.

The block isn't regular. Slope, tight access, an awkward orientation or an easement in the wrong spot. A plan drawn for a flat estate lot can be made to work on a difficult block, but the making-it-work is where the money goes, and it goes into earthworks and retaining rather than into the house.

The family doesn't fit the plan. Three kids and a grandparent, or working from home, or a block whose good view is at the back and whose living areas end up at the front. These are the things that make a house feel wrong for years and are almost free to fix at the drawing stage.

The block has something worth designing around. On the Bayside that's often the aspect — where the light comes from, where the breeze comes from in February, where you can see water from. A plan selected from a range faces whichever way it faces.

What questions are worth asking, either way?

These work on any builder, including us. Take them to every conversation.

  1. What exactly is in this number, and what is not? Ask specifically about demolition, asbestos removal, the soil test and the engineering that follows it, approvals, connecting services, driveway, fencing and landscaping.
  2. What happens to the price after the soil test? Every builder has an answer. You want to hear it before you sign, not after.
  3. Who is on the phone in month four? Get a name, and ask how many jobs that person is running.
  4. Is home warranty insurance included? In Queensland most residential building work over $3,300 including materials, labour and GST must be covered under the Queensland Home Warranty Scheme, and the premium forms part of the contract price. If it isn't mentioned, ask why.
  5. Can I check your licence? The QBCC publishes a licence search. Anyone legitimate will tell you to go and look. Ours is 15486557.
  6. Can I talk to the last family you built for? Not a testimonial. A phone number.

A builder who answers all six without flinching is one worth dealing with, whatever kind of build they're offering.

Common questions

Is a package cheaper than a custom build?
Often the advertised number is lower, and the two numbers usually aren't measuring the same thing. Compare the inclusion lists rather than the totals, and ask both builders what moves after the soil test.

Can I put a package house on a sloping block?
Usually yes, with site works. The question is what those site works cost on your particular slope, and whether that money would have bought you a better house if it had been designed for the slope in the first place.

Do I have to use the builder's demolisher?
Ask. Some packages include demolition, some allow for it, and some leave it to you.

Does a custom build take longer?
There's a design stage, so there's more time before anything is built. What that time buys is a house that suits the block and the family. Whether that trade is worth it is genuinely your call.

We already have plans. Can you build them?
Yes, that's a normal thing to ask a builder. Bring them to the walk-through.
