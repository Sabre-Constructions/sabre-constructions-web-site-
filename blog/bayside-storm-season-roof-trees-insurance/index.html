Bayside storm season: what it means for
your roof, your trees and your insurance.

Every October the afternoons get heavy, and every Bayside house gets tested. Here's what council and the state say to check before the season, what a builder actually looks at on a seventy-year-old house, and why the first big storm is often the day a family decides about the house.

Nathan Brain, carpenter and builder at Sabre 17 September 2026 6 min read

The short answer

Brisbane City Council puts storm season at October to March, and tells you to prepare for heavy rain, strong winds and hail. On the Bayside add the salt-laden wind off the bay, and the trees.

Before it starts, the state's Get Ready Queensland checklist is short: check the roof and repair it; keep gutters and downpipes clear; trim trees and branches near the buildings; fix rust, loose fittings and rotting or termite-affected timber; secure loose items; check the insurance covers the house and contents.

On an old Bayside house, three of those six are the whole story. Roofing screws and gutters rust in salt air. Timber rots where the paint failed. And the roof is the part nobody has looked at in years. Storm season is when a house tells you the truth about itself.

When is storm season, and what does it bring?

“Storm season typically lasts from October to March. During this time, prepare for potential heavy rain, strong winds, hail and other severe weather conditions.”

Brisbane City Council, Brisbane Severe Weather Alert. Council runs an alert service for severe storms and flooding in the Brisbane area; sign up before October, not during it.

On the Bayside the storms arrive from the west and south-west in the late afternoon, and the wind that follows comes off the bay carrying salt. That combination — a hot day, a black sky at four o'clock, hail, then a night of salt wind — is the Bayside summer. Houses here are built for it or they aren't.

What does the state say to check?

“Check the condition of your roof and repair if needed.” “Keep gutters and downpipes clear of leaves and other blockages.” “Trim trees and branches that are close to your buildings.” “Fix any rust, loose fittings and rotting or termite-affected timber.” “Secure loose items that could cause damage if blown around in high winds.” “Make sure to check your insurance policy has enough cover for your home and contents.”

Get Ready Queensland, Protect your home, Queensland Government. Six lines. On a post-war Bayside house, the first and the fourth are the ones that cost money.

What does a builder look at on an old Bayside roof?

The state says “check the roof.” Here's what that means when the roof is sixty years old and three streets from the bay.

  • The screws and the sheets. Salt air corrodes roofing screws from the head down. A sheet that's held by rust is a sheet that lifts in a gust.
  • The gutters. Old steel gutters rust through at the joins and behind the fascia, where nobody sees it until the water is running down the inside of the wall.
  • The flashings. Around the chimney, the vent pipes, the change of pitch. The places a roof leaks are rarely the middle of a sheet.
  • The battens and the frame. Under the sheets, in a hardwood-framed house, the battens may be fine at seventy. Where the roof has leaked for years, they aren't.
  • Tiles. On a 1970s brick house, cracked tiles, slipped ridge capping and mortar that's gone to sand. Hail finds all three.

A roof plumber can fix most of this. What they can't fix is a roof that's at the end of its life on a house that's at the end of its life, and that's the conversation storm season starts.

What about the trees?

The Bayside's old suburbs have old trees, and the state's advice is to trim the ones close to the buildings. Two things to know locally.

  • Some trees are protected. Brisbane City Council has vegetation protection rules, and some trees on private land can't be removed without approval. Check before you cut, not after.
  • The tree that falls is usually the neighbour's. Talk to them in September, not in the dark in December.

If you're planning a rebuild, the trees on the block are one of the first things a builder and a designer look at — what stays, what's protected, what the new house has to be designed around.

What about insurance?

We're builders, not insurers, so this is short. The state's checklist says make sure the policy has enough cover for the house and contents. Two things a builder would add:

  • Insure the rebuild cost, not the purchase price. On the Bayside the land is most of the value; the house is what the insurer pays to replace. Ask your insurer how they set the sum insured.
  • Tell them what the house is. Age, construction, roof type, whether it's been rewired. A new house designed to current standards and flood levels is a different risk from a 1960s house that wasn't, and it's worth saying so.

Why the storm is often the decision

Here's the pattern. A family has been meaning to do something about the house for years. Then a storm lifts a sheet, or the gutter gives up and the water comes through the ceiling, or the tree comes down on the carport. The insurer sends someone. The someone says the roof needs replacing, and while they're up there, the frame under it has been wet for a decade.

Now the family is pricing a new roof on a small old house with fibro walls and one bathroom, on a block that's worth more than the house. And they ask the question that was always coming: is this the house we want to spend that on?

Sometimes yes. A sound hardwood house with a new roof has decades in it. Sometimes no, and the storm has just told them what a builder would have. Why that answer is so common on the Bayside is here. Either way, the honest thing is to get the whole house looked at, not just the roof, before the money goes anywhere.

Key takeaways

  • October to March, every year. Heavy rain, wind, hail — plus salt wind off the bay.
  • The state's list is six lines: roof, gutters, trees, rust and rot, loose items, insurance.
  • On an old Bayside house the roof is the story. Screws, gutters, flashings, and what's under them.
  • Check before you cut a tree. Some are protected, and the one that falls is usually next door's.
  • The storm often starts the house decision. Get the whole house looked at before the roof money goes anywhere.

Common questions

When should we do the checks?

September, before council's October start. Roof plumbers and arborists are booked out once the first storm has been through.

Can we clean the gutters ourselves?

On a lowset, many people do. On a highset, it's a ladder job at height, and on a pre-1990 house the eaves may be fibro — don't drill, cut or pressure-wash them. The asbestos rules are here.

Does a new house handle storms better?

It's built to the current wind, water and flood requirements, with new fixings and flashings, and designed for the block. That's not a guarantee against a big storm; it's a different starting point.

Should we wait until after storm season to start a build?

Ask your builder how they sequence the roof. A house that's framed and roofed before the wet is a different job from one that's open to it, and that's a scheduling conversation, not a rule.

Sources: Brisbane City Council, Brisbane Severe Weather Alert; Get Ready Queensland, Protect your home, Queensland Government. Read 17 September 2026. The roof and tree observations are a builder's, not an inspection of any particular house. Not insurance, arboricultural or legal advice.
