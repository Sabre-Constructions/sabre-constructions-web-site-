Your questions answered · Knockdown rebuild

Can we keep the pool, the shed or the
front fence in a knockdown rebuild?

Nobody wants to pay to demolish a pool they like. Whether it can stay is a real question with a real answer, and the answer is different for the pool, the shed, the fence and the driveway. Here's what decides each one.

Nathan Brain, carpenter and builder at Sabre 17 September 2026 5 min read

The short answer

Sometimes. A pool can often stay if it's sound, it isn't where the new house or the machinery needs to be, and the new design is drawn around it. A shed can stay if it's clear of the work and legally there. A front fence can stay if it isn't in the way of the crossover and the deliveries. A driveway usually goes, because the new house rarely sits where the old one did.

What decides it is position, condition, access and rules. Position: is it where the house, the excavator or the crane needs to be? Condition: is it worth keeping? Access: can the job happen around it? Rules: pool fencing, boundary setbacks and council's requirements still apply to anything that stays.

Keeping something isn't free. Protecting a pool through a demolition and a build is work, and a kept fence can cost more in slow deliveries than a new one. Ask for the honest comparison.

Can the pool stay?

This is the one people care about most, and the one with the most conditions.

  • Position. If the pool sits where the new house goes, or where the excavator has to pass to get to the back, it goes. If it's in a corner the design can leave alone, it can stay.
  • Condition. A concrete pool with a sound shell and working plumbing is worth keeping. A fibreglass shell that's lifted, or a pool with old pipework you can't trace, is a liability under a new house.
  • The work around it. Demolition next to a pool means protecting the shell from machinery, keeping rubble out of it, and dealing with a big hole full of water on a construction site for months. It's done; it's planned for.
  • The rules. Pool safety barriers are law in Queensland and the new fence and the new house have to meet them. A kept pool is a design input like the floor level: the barrier, the doors and the windows facing it all have to comply.

Can the shed stay?

Usually the easiest yes, if two things are true: it's out of the way, and it was approved.

A shed at the back corner, clear of the house footprint and the machinery path, can be left alone and worked around. A shed that was never approved, or that sits inside a setback, can become a problem when the certifier looks at the whole site for the new house. Council's demolition page notes you don't need planning approval to demolish a free-standing outbuilding built after 1946, which is often the simpler path if the shed is marginal.

Can the front fence stay?

Often, and it depends on the crossover.

The trucks, the excavator and the frame delivery come through the front. If the existing crossover and gate are wide enough and in the right place for the new driveway, the fence can stay and be protected. If the new house moves the driveway, a section comes out and goes back later. A heritage-style timber fence on an old Wynnum street is worth the trouble; a tired paling fence usually isn't.

What about the driveway and retaining walls?

The driveway almost always goes. The new house rarely sits exactly where the old one did, and the garage rarely lands where the carport was. The old slab is broken up with the rest.

Retaining walls are different. A sound engineered wall holding a sloped Bayside block can be the most valuable thing on the site, and the design keeps it. A stack of sleepers leaning outward is not a retaining wall for a new house's purposes, and the engineer will say so.

What does keeping something cost?

Ask for it as a comparison, not a yes or no. Keeping a pool means protection, slower machinery access, and design constraints. Keeping a fence can mean a section removed and reinstated. Sometimes the honest answer is that demolishing and replacing is cheaper than protecting, and sometimes it isn't. A builder should be able to tell you which before the design is finished.

Key takeaways

  • Position, condition, access, rules. Those four decide everything in the yard.
  • A sound pool out of the machinery's way can stay, and the new house is designed to the pool barrier laws around it.
  • An approved shed clear of the work stays easily. An unapproved one is simpler gone.
  • The fence depends on the crossover. The driveway almost always goes.
  • Keeping isn't free. Ask for the comparison, not the wish.

Common questions

Does a kept pool need a new fence?

Whatever is around it at the end has to meet Queensland's pool safety laws, and the new house's doors and windows facing the pool are part of that. Usually the barrier is redone as part of the landscaping. The pool safety certificate is checked at the end.

Can we keep the old carport?

Only if it's clear of the new house, structurally sound and legally there. Most aren't all three. It's often the first thing to go, because it's where the site sheds and the deliveries end up.

What about the old mango tree by the pool?

Trees are a different rule. If it's mature and prominent it may be protected under council's local law regardless of what you'd like. That post is here.

Who decides what stays?

You do, with the facts. The builder tells you what's possible and what it costs; the designer draws around it; the certifier and the engineer check that what stays meets the rules.
