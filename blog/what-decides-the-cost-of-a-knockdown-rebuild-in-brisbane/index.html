What decides the cost of a
knockdown rebuild in Brisbane?

Every calculator on the internet gives you a number for this, and every one of them is guessing, because none of them has stood on your block. Here is what actually moves the cost of a knockdown rebuild, in the order a builder thinks about it. No figures — the honest version doesn't have any until someone has walked your site.

Nathan Brain, carpenter and builder at Sabre 17 September 2026 6 min read

The short answer

The cost of a knockdown rebuild is decided by nine things, and only two of them are the ones people expect: the size of the new house and the level of finish. The other seven belong to the old house and the block, and they're the reason two families with the same floor plan end up with very different numbers.

They are: what the old house is made of and what's in it (asbestos), how it comes down, the slope and access of the block, which council overlays apply (character, flood), what the soil test says, how the new house meets the ground, and where you live while it happens.

Anyone who gives you a figure before they've checked those seven is quoting the house, not the job. We don't publish a price for the same reason we don't guess your floor level: the block decides it, and a walk-through takes an hour.

Why won't anyone give a straight number?

Because the honest ones can't yet, and the others are quoting something else.

A “from” price is the price of a house on a flat, clear, dry lot with nothing in the way. Most Bayside blocks are not that. They have a seventy-year-old house on them, a slope toward the water, a tree council cares about, and a flood level in the FloodWise report.

Each of those adds work that has to be done before the new house exists. That's why the same plan lands differently on different blocks, and why the quote you can trust is the one written after someone has stood on yours.

“Allowances (items or services for which the price is not fixed when the contract is signed) should be kept to a minimum as the final cost often exceeds the estimate in the contract.”

Queensland Building and Construction Commission, Consumer Building Guide, version 3, effective July 2023. The regulator's own warning: a price with a lot of allowances in it isn't a price yet.

What does the old house add?

Three things, and the first is the one nobody budgets for.

  • What it's made of. Most Bayside houses from before 1990 have asbestos somewhere: fibro sheeting, eaves, the bathroom, the built-in-under. Licensed removal happens before demolition and is its own job with its own paperwork. What that involves is here.
  • How it comes down. A small post-war cottage on a clear block is a short job for a demolition contractor. A big highset wedged between two neighbours, with a pool and a slab underneath, is not.
  • What's under and around it. Old slabs, old footings, old sheds, buried rubbish, a septic tank from before the sewer came through. It all has to leave before anyone can dig.

What does the block decide?

Two things above all: slope and access.

Slope decides how the house meets the ground. A flat block takes a slab. A block that falls toward the bay takes cut and fill, retaining, or a highset design, and each of those is real work. It's also why a flat-block package plan doesn't fit — that post is here.

Access decides what machinery can get in and how materials arrive. A narrow frontage on a quiet Wynnum street with power lines down the front and a neighbour's fence a metre from the boundary changes how everything is delivered and lifted.

Then the smaller ones: where the sewer and stormwater run, whether the block is a corner, where the street trees are, and what the neighbours have built to the boundary.

What do the overlays and the ground do?

Council's overlays don't usually stop a rebuild on the Bayside. They add design work and sometimes an application.

  • The character overlay. If the house was built in 1946 or earlier and the street is in the overlay, demolition needs a development application before anything else. The rules are here.
  • The flood overlays. They set a minimum habitable floor level. That can push the design highset, which is a different house from a lowset one. What that changes is here.
  • The soil test. A geotechnical report classifies your ground under the Australian Standard for residential slabs and footings. Sand and rock need simple footings. Reactive clay needs engineered ones. The letter on that report moves the price of everything below the floor.
  • Protected trees. A tree council protects under its local law stays, and the design works around it. That is not a small thing on a block with one big gum in the middle.

What about the new house itself?

This is the part people expect, and it still matters: size, storeys, and finish.

Size is the obvious one. But shape counts too — a long thin house has more wall for the same floor than a square one, and a two-storey house has a smaller, cheaper roof and slab for the same rooms than a sprawling single storey.

Finish is where the widest range lives. The same room can be fitted out plainly or expensively, and the difference is entirely the family's choice. That's the one lever you control completely, and the one we'd rather you spend your decisions on than on things the block has already decided.

What does living somewhere else cost the job?

It isn't in the contract, and it's part of the cost. During a knockdown rebuild the family lives somewhere else, and rent or a relative's spare room for the length of the build belongs in the budget beside the house. The length of that build is a question for the builder you choose, in writing, with a date for practical completion in the contract. Ask for it.

Key takeaways

  • Nine things move the cost, and seven belong to the old house and the block, not the new house.
  • Asbestos, slope, access and the soil test are the four that surprise people most.
  • Overlays usually change the design, not whether you can build. Check character and flood first.
  • Finish is the one lever you fully control. Spend your decisions there.
  • A price with allowances in it isn't a price yet — the QBCC's words, not ours.
  • The number comes from the block. Nobody honest has it before they've stood on it.

Common questions

Why won't Sabre put a starting price on the website?

Because we'd be quoting a flat, clear lot, and we don't build on many of those. A number that changes the moment we see your block isn't useful to you, and we'd rather be the builder who told you what moves it.

Is a two-storey house always dearer than a single storey?

Not for the same rooms. A two-storey house sits on a smaller slab under a smaller roof, and on a sloping or small block it's often the sensible shape. What costs more is the extra floor area, not the extra floor.

What should we have ready before we ask for a quote?

The year the house was built, council's FloodWise report on the lot, and a rough idea of the rooms you need. Everything else Nathan reads off the block at the walk-through.

Will the quote have allowances in it?

Some items can't be fixed until they're chosen or checked — that's what an allowance is for. The QBCC's guidance is to keep them to a minimum. Ask any builder to point to each one and say why it's there.
