Is Wynnum a good place to live?
An honest look at the good and the annoying.

Every guide to Wynnum is written by someone selling a house in it. This one is written by a family that builds them, and has since 1990. Here's what's good, what's annoying, and what the houses are actually like once you're inside one.

Nathan Brain, carpenter and builder at Sabre 17 September 2026 6 min read

The short answer

Yes, for most families, and for reasons that don't change: the bay at the end of the street, a train to the city, a village centre you can walk to, and a stock of family-sized blocks that most of Brisbane ran out of decades ago.

The annoying parts are real too. The tide goes out a long way, so the water isn't always where the postcard says. Salt air works on everything. Storm season is a season. And most of the houses were built between the 1940s and the 1970s, which means they're small, tired, or both.

That last one is why we're allowed to write this. Wynnum's problem is rarely the suburb. It's the house, on a block that's worth staying on.

Who actually lives in Wynnum?

Families, mostly, and people who grew up here and never saw a reason to leave.

In the 2021 Census, Wynnum had 14,036 people with a median age of 43. Couple families with children were 43.0% of families, and 70.8% of dwellings were separate houses.

Australian Bureau of Statistics, 2021 Census QuickStats, Wynnum (Qld). Seven in ten homes are still stand-alone houses on their own block. That's the number that matters to a builder.

Wynnum was a town before it was a suburb. It had its own shire from 1902 and a town council with its own mayor a dozen years later, and you can still feel it: a main street, a jetty, a memorial park, a high school that's been there since the 1940s. It's a place with a centre, not a subdivision with a shopping centre.

What's good about it?

  • The water is the end of the street. The esplanade runs the length of the suburb. The wading pool, the jetty, Pandanus Beach and the parklands are where families end up on a Sunday whether they planned to or not.
  • The train. Three stations in the suburb on the Cleveland line, and a straight run to the city. How far it really is, with a real timetable, is here.
  • A village you can walk to. Bay Terrace and Edith Street still work as a main street. Coffee, a hardware shop, the doctor, the pub.
  • Schools inside the suburb. A state primary, a state high school and independent options within a few kilometres. The catchments are here.
  • The blocks. Flat, regular, on old street grids. The kind of land that's almost gone from the inner suburbs.
  • Sixteen kilometres of shoreline. The Bayside Parklands run along mangroves, mudflats and wetlands that are part of a Ramsar-listed bay. Migratory shorebirds arrive every year. It's not a marina; it's a coast.

What's annoying about it?

A fair page says this part out loud.

  • The tide goes out. A long way. At low tide the water off the esplanade is mudflats to the horizon. If you moved here for swimming off the beach, you'll be swimming at the wading pool. If you moved here for the light and the birds, it's the best part.
  • Salt. Screws rust, paint goes chalky, aluminium pits, the windward side of the house ages faster than the lee. Every house on the bay is in a slow argument with the air.
  • Storm season. October to March, most years. Trees down, gutters overflowing, the odd hailstorm. What it means for a house is here.
  • The flats. Nearly three in ten dwellings are now units or townhouses, and the esplanade end of the suburb has changed. If you want the old Wynnum, it's a few streets back.
  • The houses. Which is the next section.

What are the houses like?

Old. That isn't a complaint; it's a date.

Brisbane's suburbs “became populated with low-set timber houses on quarter acre blocks” in the years after 1946, when a Brisbane architect estimated a national shortfall of 300,000 houses.

State Library of Queensland, Housing a growing state, 2014. Wynnum got its share. The post-war cottage on a flat block near the water is the suburb's basic unit.

Walk a street two back from the esplanade and you'll see the whole set: a pre-war timber house on stumps, a post-war fibro cottage, a 1960s highset with the rooms built in underneath, a 1970s brick lowset. What each one is made of, and what that means, is here.

What they have in common is that they were built for a family of the 1950s, on a budget of the 1950s, with the materials of the 1950s. Two or three bedrooms, one bathroom, a kitchen at the back, and fibro somewhere. Sixty or seventy years on, they're at the point where the next round of work — restumping, rewiring, a roof, a second bathroom — costs real money and still leaves you with a small old house.

Which is fine, if the house fits. It's the families it doesn't fit that end up ringing us.

Stay or go: the question the suburb keeps asking

Here's the pattern we see, over and over. A family buys a small house in Wynnum because the street is right. Ten years on, the street is still right — the school, the neighbours, the walk to the water — and the house has run out of room.

The choice is a bigger house in a suburb they don't want, or a different house on the block they already love. On a good flat Wynnum block, the second option is often the honest one, and it's why there are so many demolition signs on so many fences. The reasons are here.

Not every house should come down. Some should be raised and opened up. Some just need a kitchen. The point is that in Wynnum, the suburb almost never turns out to be the problem.

Key takeaways

  • Yes, it's a good place to live, and the reasons are permanent: the bay, the train, the village, the blocks.
  • The annoying parts are real: the tide goes out, the salt eats things, storm season is a season, and the esplanade has gone to units.
  • Seven in ten homes are still separate houses — ABS 2021 — and most of them are post-war.
  • The houses are the problem, not the suburb. Small, old, fibro somewhere, on blocks worth keeping.
  • Families who outgrow the house rarely outgrow the street. That's the whole reason we're here.

Common questions

Is Wynnum safe?

We're builders, not the police, so we won't quote a number we didn't measure. The Queensland Police Service publishes crime statistics by suburb online; read them for Wynnum and the suburbs you're comparing it with, over a few years rather than one.

Can you swim at Wynnum?

In the tidal wading pool on the esplanade, yes, and at Pandanus Beach when the tide is in. Off the foreshore at low tide, no — it's mud to the horizon. That's Moreton Bay, not a fault.

Is Wynnum expensive?

Prices move, and anything we printed would be wrong by the time you read it. Ask an agent for last month's sales, not us. What we can say is that the value on the Bayside sits in the land, which is why so many families rebuild rather than move.

Does Wynnum flood?

Parts of it are mapped for storm tide and overland flow, and council publishes a report for every lot. What the maps actually show is here.
